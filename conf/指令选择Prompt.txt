# CATALOG AUTHORITY NOTE

The Suite, Package, and Module descriptions are the authoritative module catalog. The Routing Principles and Tie-Breaker Rules provide cross-cutting decision logic, precedence rules, exceptions, and output constraints; do not treat the routing rules as a replacement for the module descriptions.


# ROUTING PRINCIPLES AND TIE-BREAKER RULES

A. Routing Algorithm

For each research scope, internally route through Suite → Package → Module, but output only the selected Module_ID in R3. Select the module by evaluating three factors in order: primary evidence/source environment or evidence basis, primary analytic intent, and required deliverable form. For analytic, consumer, quantitative, e-commerce, survey, and document-synthesis scopes, evidence/source fit is primary and deliverable fit is required. For prescriptive scopes, the decisive factor is whether the user asks for a recommendation, strategy, design, plan, roadmap, playbook, governance model, mitigation plan, response design, or executive decision.

Each X1 must have one dominant evidence/source environment or evidence basis, one dominant analytic intent, and one dominant deliverable form. If a potential X1 combines materially different outputs, split it into separate scopes. Examples: split consumer-demand diagnosis from strategy design; split market sizing from market-entry design; split risk identification from risk mitigation; split revenue modeling from investment recommendation; split specialized document synthesis from downstream recommendation when the document synthesis is a substantive prerequisite.

If multiple modules plausibly fit, choose the module whose evidence assumptions and core output structure most directly match the intended deliverable, not merely the topic domain. Prefer the most specific fitting module over a broad core module. Use a broad core module only when the sub-scope is genuinely general, cross-cutting, legacy-compatible, or not clearly captured by a more specific module.


B. X1 Scope Discipline

Do not split a coherent research question merely because more modules are available. Split only when each X1 has a distinct evidence need, analytic intent, and deliverable. Merge potential scopes that use the same evidence base, answer the same sub-question, or produce substantially duplicative conclusions.

Normally create more than 2 and between 6 and 15 X1 scopes for broad research queries. For genuinely narrow queries, create the minimum number of substantive X1 scopes required, but never create artificial X1 solely to satisfy a target count. Order X1 from prerequisite to downstream: define/map first, then source-grounded synthesis where needed, then compare/diagnose, then explain/model, then evaluate/recommend/design, then implement/govern/mitigate where applicable.


C. Core Suite Special Rules

Use the Core Multi-Source Research Suite as the foundational and fallback suite when a sub-scope is broad, general, legacy-compatible, cross-cutting, or not clearly captured by a more specific suite/module.

Use G21 only when the sub-scope requires a comprehensive analytic overview of a specific industry, company, brand, market, technology, policy area, or other defined topic. Do NOT use G21 to synthesize outputs from other routed research scopes, and do NOT create a final G21 synthesis scope by default.

Use G20 only for e-commerce performance questions involving online brand/product/SKU/category performance, platform-commerce momentum, online channels, campaigns, price bands, promotion windows, seasonality, online sales drivers, or e-commerce growth diagnostics. Do NOT use G20 for offline retail, physical store performance, general brand perception, social sentiment, or general market analysis.

Use G22 only when the input already contains a GPT-compressed consumer panel or response context representing many unique consumers and the task is to convert it into countable distributions, ratios, chart-ready segments, or quantified consumer insights. Do NOT use G22 for raw social media, raw reviews, new survey design, or general qualitative consumer analysis.


D. Specialized Document Synthesis Rules

Use G92–G97 when a sub-scope is primarily a structured synthesis of a specialized document corpus as a document type, OR when specialized source materials are the most important evidence basis and a source-grounded extraction/synthesis scope would materially improve the overall research plan. These modules are stronger than other modules for deeply analyzing litigation materials, tender documents, academic papers, policy documents, patent or technical innovation documents, and news/event corpora.

If the user query involves a broader task that depends on specialized source materials, consider creating one or more prerequisite X1 scopes using the relevant G92–G97 module before downstream comparison, causal analysis, quantitative modeling, risk assessment, design, or recommendation scopes. For example, a regulatory response project may first require policy-document synthesis using G95 before policy-response strategy design; a patent landscape project may first require patent/technical innovation synthesis using G96 before opportunity analysis or strategy design; a litigation-risk project may first require case-material synthesis using G92 before risk or mitigation analysis.

Use G92 for litigation/adjudication/dispute-resolution materials; G93 for tender/bidding/procurement/award materials; G94 for academic papers or scholarly literature; G95 for policy/regulation/governance/public-administration documents; G96 for patents, technical disclosures, invention documents, R&D materials, or technical innovation records; and G97 for news articles, current events, media coverage, event clusters, or evolving topical narratives.

Do NOT use G92–G97 merely because the topic touches law, procurement, academia, policy, patents, or news. If there is no specialized corpus, no source-grounded document synthesis need, and the sub-scope is better framed as comparison, risk assessment, causal explanation, quantitative modeling, consumer-demand analysis, or prescriptive design, choose the narrower non-document module instead.


E. Consumer Demand-Side Analytics Rules

Use consumer/social modules only when all three conditions are likely true: the topic is consumer-, shopper-, brand-, product-experience-, service-experience-, or category-perception-facing; the question asks for demand-side behavior, perception, motivation, friction, sentiment, category meaning, brand meaning, purchase logic, loyalty, or opportunity signals; and there is likely enough available or provided consumer verbatim evidence from social media, reviews, UGC, comments, short-form content, communities, or brand-owned content.

The purpose is not to analyze social media as a platform or media channel. The purpose is to use consumer verbatims as evidence for demand-side analytics. If probable consumer-verbatim evidence volume is weak, prefer a general professional-evidence module unless the user explicitly supplies the verbatim corpus or asks to use social/review evidence.

Prefer the Consumer Discourse Intelligence Suite when the demand-side question is granular, such as motivation, usage context, product perception, product friction, service friction, brand meaning, marketing response, persuasion, controversy, trend adoption, category boundaries, purchase hesitation, loyalty, or market gaps from consumer verbatims. Use Core Consumer modules for broader or simpler consumer tasks such as broad profiling, concept/vocabulary analysis, sentiment diagnosis, decision journey mapping, or product design dimension analysis.


F. Quantitative and General Analytical Rules

Use the Quantitative Economics & Modeling Suite when the sub-scope requires explicit numerical modeling, market sizing, demand forecasting, adoption modeling, installed-base or replacement modeling, revenue build, pricing, monetization, cost structure, unit economics, margin pool, valuation, capacity, throughput, utilization, capex, operating leverage, retail economics, or industry-specific operating economics. Use Core G23 only for generic calculations, simple ratios, rough scenario arithmetic, cross-checking assumptions, or quantitative outputs that do not fit a more specific quantitative module.

Use the General Research Reasoning Suite when the sub-scope is analytical and primarily supported by professional, market, company, policy, technical, operational, or public evidence, but is not primarily demand-side consumer-verbatim analytics, not explicit quantitative modeling, not specialized document-corpus synthesis, and not prescriptive design. Use its design-action modules only for broad or lightweight action design; use the Strategic Decision & Execution Design Suite for specialized prescriptive business, market, investment, operating, governance, crisis, consumer-goods, retail, or execution design.


G. Prescriptive Design Rules

Use the Strategic Decision & Execution Design Suite only for prescriptive outputs. Select these modules when the user asks what should be done, how to design something, how to respond, how to enter a market, how to position, how to launch, how to allocate resources, how to govern, how to mitigate risks, whether to invest, whether to proceed, whether to acquire, or how to implement.

Do NOT select a design or recommendation module merely because the topic is strategic. Select design/recommendation modules only when the requested deliverable is prescriptive.

A user request may require multiple design modules as separate, sequential X1 scopes when the overall task contains distinct prescriptive layers. For example, an end-to-end market-entry plan may require separate scopes for market-entry strategy, competitive positioning, channel strategy, launch design, implementation roadmap, and governance. Do not force multiple design scopes unless each has distinct evidence needs, design outputs, or implementation dependencies.


H. Dependency Rules

Set dependencies according to research logic, not module order alone. Descriptive and evidence-structuring scopes are usually independent or prerequisite. Specialized document synthesis scopes are often prerequisite when downstream scopes depend on facts, claims, chronology, evidence, policy mechanisms, technical disclosures, paper findings, tender requirements, or news developments extracted from the source corpus. Causal explanation usually depends on timeline, system mapping, comparison, source-grounded synthesis, or diagnosis when present. Quantitative modeling usually depends on market definition, scope boundaries, assumptions, input data, source-grounded synthesis, or prior mapping when present. Valuation and investment-return analysis usually depend on revenue, cost, margin, cash-flow, market-demand, or operating assumptions when present.

Prescriptive design, recommendation, implementation, governance, mitigation, and response scopes usually depend on prior evidence, source-grounded synthesis, diagnosis, comparison, risk assessment, consumer-demand analysis, or quantitative modeling when present. Risk mitigation usually depends on risk identification or vulnerability assessment. Implementation roadmap usually depends on a chosen strategy, recommendation, design direction, or prioritized initiative set. Governance and monitoring usually depend on implementation design, operating model design, or execution priorities.


I. Q2, Q2_Rev, and R3 Output Rules

Q2 and Q2_Rev must preserve single-module fit. Do not let Q2_Rev expand into adjacent deliverables that would require another module. If expansion would require another analytic nature, split the scope instead.

Q2 and Q2_Rev must be written as research scopes, not routing labels. They must not explicitly refer to any Module_ID, exact Module_Name as a routing label, Package_Name, Suite_Name, or analytic suite/package/module terminology.

R3 must output exactly one Module_ID for each Q2. Never output multiple Module_IDs for one Q2. Never output Suite_Name, Package_Name, Module_Name, explanation, or description in R3.
